196619128.irrationalities

July 31, 2026 6 min read Your Name

There is something deeply weird happening to the market right now. You smell it, I smell it, anyone paying attention to the broader economy smells it. We just can’t quite put our finger on the pulse of it.

Things are undeniably messy on a global scale. We are watching geopolitical tensions boil, inflation stick around long enough, and the cost of living squeeze the middle class into a fine powder. And yet, amidst this global anxiety, a phenomenal contradiction is playing out: the U.S. stock market keeps touching new, dizzying all-time highs.

  • Everyone is confused by the sheer irrationality of it all.

But if you look closely at the “smartest” money in the room, they aren’t partying. Look at Warren Buffett. Berkshire Hathaway is currently sitting on nearly $400 billion in cash. That is an unprecedented pile of liquid money.

To put that $400 billion into perspective, let’s do some quick geopolitical math. If Berkshire Hathaway were a country, its cash pile alone would have a higher GDP than the entire nations of Finland ($300B) or New Zealand ($250B). Buffett could theoretically buy the entire economic output of New Zealand, hand them the keys back, and still have enough leftover cash to buy out the entire economy of Kuwait.

When the greatest investor of our time is hoarding the equivalent of sovereign GDPs in cash while the market rips to all-time highs, it signals a profound defensive posture. It means the underlying plumbing of the market is broken.

It hit me recently that there are two major events driving this invisible fracture—two mechanics that are likely the first of their kind, and perhaps the last.

1. Yen Changed

The first major hit is the Japanese Central Bank finally raising interest rates.

To understand why this matters, you have to understand the “Yen Carry Trade.” For decades, Japan has kept its interest rates at exactly zero, or even negative. This created a massive loophole for institutional investors around the world. Wall Street would borrow Japanese Yen for practically nothing, convert those Yen into U.S. Dollars, and buy high-yielding U.S. tech stocks. It was free money.

But when Japan raised rates, the music stopped. Suddenly, the Yen became more valuable, meaning the debt these investors owed in Japan became vastly more expensive to pay back. To cover these massive, unexpected margins, funds were forced to liquidate their U.S. assets.

This creates a long-tail effect that drains liquidity from the global system. It’s an invisible deleveraging. The money that artificially propped up valuations for the last decade is being repatriated back to Tokyo. When the cost of capital fundamentally shifts like this, the old models break.

2. The Algorithmic Machine

The second event is the sheer dominance of algorithmic trading, for the last 15 years at least.

We are no longer trading against humans reading balance sheets; we are trading against machines reading momentum. These algorithms don’t care about a company’s P/E ratio, its leadership team, or its 10-year vision. They care about order flow, moving averages, and options hedging.

Here is how these two forces interact to amplify chaos: When a real-world event happens—like the unwinding of the Yen Carry Trade causing a slight dip in the market—the algorithms don’t pause to assess the macroeconomic landscape. They instantly sense the downward momentum and auto-trigger mass sell-offs. They amplify a 2% fundamental correction into a 6% flash crash.

Conversely, on the way up, algorithms chase each other’s tails. They see buying momentum and buy more, pushing valuations to absurd, unjustifiable all-time highs simply because the line is going up. The market is completely detached from reality because the machines are trading the trend, not the truth.

This is a new world, and it is a strange one. But as strange and pessimistic as the short-term mechanics of the market are, there is a broader philosophical lesson I’ve been thinking about lately.

On Leadership

I was recently listening to a podcast featuring Naval Ravikant (Nothing Ever Happens is Over), and they touched on something interesting. We are living in strange times, and navigating them requires an optimism that is deeply tied to creativity.

People easily understand bad things as they happen. A pessimistic worldview is cheap; it requires very little imagination. But a better worldview—a hopeful one—requires you to actually sit down and build a future in your mind.

To quote the podcast directly:

“For example, the job loss thing is a clear example. It’s very easy to look at existing jobs and see how they will go away, but it’s very hard to predict what the next job will be. Yet inevitably there’s always a next job. Because of that, I think people tend to fixate on the doom scenarios. It’s much easier to imagine the methods of doom than to imagine the methods of rising up.”

This is brilliant. Pessimism is just drawing a straight line downward from current trends. When AI arrives, it is incredibly easy to imagine a copywriter losing their job. It requires intense, exhausting creativity to imagine the entirely new industries, economies, and professions that will be born from the ashes of the old ones.

The conversation continued:

“But I think it’s very hard to imagine creativity. It’s very hard to be optimistic. And so I think we have to nurture optimism. We have to reward optimism. We have to be irrationally optimistic, because that’s the only way out of this anyway. So whenever people do the crabs in a bucket thing where they try to pull the optimists back down and they keep saying, ‘Doom, doom, doom,’ they might be right, but it’s certainly not helping matters. That’s not the person you want to be in a foxhole with.”

That last line stuck with me. That’s not the person you want to be in a foxhole with.

The foxholes, the trenches, the frontiers—these are the ultimate tests of character. I don’t appreciate people who back down in hardship, but I do appreciate people who tell the truth, even when the truth is terrifying, and then immediately pick up a shovel to help fix it.

The doom-sayers might be technically right about the algorithmic fragility of the stock market. They might be right that the unwinding of the global carry trade is going to cause immense short-term pain.

But you cannot build a life, a company, or a legacy on doom.

The leadership philosophy I want to embody—and the kind of leaders I want to follow—is rooted in what we call the Stockdale Paradox - a psychological strategy of maintaining unwavering faith that you will prevail in the end, while simultaneously confronting the most brutal facts of your current reality. Named after Admiral Jim Stockdale, a U.S. prisoner of war in Vietnam for eight years, it highlights that realistic optimists thrive, while blind optimists fail.

Expect the worst. Tread carefully. Build a $400 billion mattress of cash if you have to. Acknowledge that the algorithms are running wild and the global debt machine is shifting. Protect your downside.

But when we look out over a 10, 20, or 30-year horizon, we must choose to be aggressively, irrationally hopeful. We must bet on human ingenuity, on the inevitability of the “next job,” and on our collective ability to rise up.
