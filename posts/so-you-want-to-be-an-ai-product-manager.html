180237310.so-you-want-to-be-an-ai-product-manager

July 31, 2026 7 min read Your Name

If you spend any time on LinkedIn, you’d think being an AI Product Manager is pure victory. It’s all about agent demos that book perfect vacations, thought leadership threads on “the GenAI revolution,” and founders-mode energy about “10x-ing everything.”

Let me tell you what it’s actually like from the inside: it’s messy, exhilarating, occasionally maddening, and held together with more duct tape than you’d believe. But at the same time, it’s exciting to tread between chaos.

The reality isn’t the polished narrative you see at conferences. It’s a daily exercise in building something genuinely new while the entire ecosystem—organizational structures, tooling, skillsets, even our mental models—needs to catch up.

  • According to S&P Global Market Intelligence, 42% of companies abandoned most of their AI initiatives in early 2025, up from just 17% the year before (Yayy).

And Big Tech, well, has gone all-in on AI. Every earnings call, every strategy memo, every quarterly objective screams “AI-first.” But here’s what nobody tells you: the tech world is fundamentally not ready for what it takes to build and ship reliable AI products at scale.

And yet—and this is the part that keeps me coming back every day—we’re the ones making it ready.

Your Stakeholder Map Becomes a Small Army

Remember 2022? The “before times”?

Shipping a feature meant coordinating three groups: Product, Engineering, and Design. We had playbooks. We had established patterns. Sprint planning actually made sense.

Today, shipping an AI feature involves what I can only describe as a small army with competing priorities:

  • Product & Engineering (still here, but now confused)

  • Responsible AI teams (because LLMs don’t come with guardrails)

  • Legal (who are rightfully terrified of liability)

  • Privacy & Security (because we’re touching user data in new ways)

  • Core Infrastructure (because nothing scales like we need it to)

  • Data Science (who speak a different language from Engineering)

  • UX Research (trying to study something that behaves differently every time)

Why the explosion? Because generative AI is probabilistic. The risk profile has fundamentally changed.

We’re running a technological revolution on Slack channels, spreadsheets, and frantic “alignment meetings” because the tooling to manage this complexity literally doesn’t exist yet, and yes, the strategic importance of everything that got “AI” in it

Typically, a “stakeholder sync”, supposed to be 30 minutes. Regularly runs 90 minutes because people negotiate trade-offs between innovation velocity and responsible deployment, between user value and legal risk, between “technically possible” and “organizationally feasible,” and “User value“

This is the cost of doing something genuinely new.

We’re All Junior Employees Now

Here’s the most humbling part of this job: nobody (Or prob 10% at best) really knows what they’re doing.

We’re all figuring it out in real-time.

For the last decade, we perfected deterministic software (For the most part). If the user clicks button A, then B happens. We knew how to write requirements. We knew how to QA. We knew how to ship with confidence.

AI is probabilistic. You cannot write a PRD that says “the LLM will write a helpful response.” You have to define “helpful.” You have to define acceptable failure modes. You have to think in terms of distributions, not guarantees. AI went crazy once in a while, who knows?

This shift has created a massive skills gap across the entire industry:

  • 70% of organizations consider AI skills mandatory (Pluralsight, 2025)

  • Two-thirds have had to abandon projects due to a lack of those exact skills

  • 45% of PMs report having to self-teach because internal training can’t keep up with model updates (Incl. me pretty much)

Nearly half of us are teaching ourselves while simultaneously trying to teach our teams, manage stakeholders, and ship products.

The mental models we built over decades of deterministic software don’t transfer cleanly. Clearly, this is the unknown unknown, but at the same time, we need to leap.

The hierarchy of expertise has flattened. The PM with six months of prompt engineering experience might know more about production readiness than the 20-year veteran.

We’re all junior employees in the face of this new paradigm. And to be honest, that’s okay.

Cool Tech Looking for Real Problems?

Walk into any strategy meeting right now, and you’ll feel the FOMO radiating from the walls.

  • “What if we added an AI agent to [literally any surface area]?”

  • “Can we use GenAI to [thing that doesn’t actually need GenAI]?”

  • “Our competitor just announced [vague AI feature], we need to respond!”

Gartner predicts that 30% of GenAI projects will be abandoned after Proof of Concept by the end of 2025 due to poor data quality, inadequate risk controls, or—and this is the big one—unclear business value.

As always, people are doing it in reverse.

PM now needs to be the “bad cop” who asks uncomfortable questions:

  • “Who is this actually for?”

  • “What problem does this solve that our existing solution doesn’t?”

  • “If the AI doesn’t work perfectly, does this feature still provide value?”

  • “Are we building this because it’s cool, or because users are asking for it?”

  • How do we monetize it?

The struggle isn’t a lack of ideas. We’re drowning in ideas. The struggle is clarifying which ideas actually matter.

For just the last 2 weeks, we might have had dozens of ideas for AI from all sides. Guess how many make it to production?

We’re in a phase of mass experimentation. Some experiments will fail. Most experiments will fail. But that’s the whole point of experiments.

Do we have a real AI PM?

To survive this chaos, the role of the Product Manager has fundamentally shifted.

We aren’t just “managing” the product anymore; we are orchestrating the intelligence. Here is how the best AI PMs are operating right now:

1. Speed

Bad PM blames AI, good PM utilizes it

We are building the machine with the machine. The days of spending a week writing a requirements document are over.

  • Drafting: Claude or ChatGPT to draft my PRDs, synthesize massive amounts of user research, and model complex edge cases in minutes.

  • Design: We aren’t waiting days for wireframes. Tools like Lovable or Figma Make allow us to generate unbelievably fast, high-fidelity mockups during the meeting itself.

  • Documentation: Meetings are recorded, transcribed, and summarized instantly. The goal isn’t just to work less; it’s to iterate fast enough to keep up with the model updates.

2. Qualityyyyyyyyy

Bad PM takes what the AI gives them, good PM knows the work and validates.

AI is the ultimate junior employee—enthusiastic, fast, and frequently wrong. The value of a PM has shifted from creation to validation. Ask: “Is this actually true? Does this solve the user problem?”

If you can’t distinguish between a hallucination and an insight, you are obsolete.

3. Gatekeeping

Bad PM lets things run wild, good PM keeps things realistic.

AI models are powerful engines, but they have no steering wheel. They don’t know our business strategy, our ethical lines, or our specific user constraints.

The PM provides the context. We are the guardrails that prevent the team from over-engineering a solution or shipping a feature that “works” technically but fails culturally. We are the ones saying, “Just because the AI can do this, doesn’t mean it should.”

4. Coordinate

Bad PM leaves people confused, good PM aligns.

For sure, people struggle to follow after a while, esp true for big organizations.

Frustrated when outputs are inconsistent; stakeholders panic when the model hallucinates.

In this environment, the PM is the Anchor. When the team is lost in the fog of “what is possible,” the PM is the one standing firm on “what is necessary.” We absorb the ambiguity so the team can focus on execution.

What This Means

If you’re considering a role in AI Product Management, know what you’re signing up for:

  • You will feel lost. Regularly.

  • You will spend more time in meetings than you’d like.

  • You will kill things you’ve worked on for months.

  • You will have to say “I don’t know” more than you’re comfortable with.

But you will also:

  • Build things that didn’t exist before.

  • See your products genuinely help people in ways that weren’t possible before 6 months ago.

  • Help write the playbook for this technology.

  • Be part of the conversation about the future.

The tech world isn’t ready for AI yet, and we’re the ones getting it ready.

And that’s the hardest job you would ever love.
