The Investment Paradox

First, let’s define the beast. What is a Venture Capitalist?

In a standard Western MBA textbook, a VC is an investor who provides capital to early-stage, high-potential startups in exchange for equity. It is the apex predator form of capitalism. It is not about steady dividends or safe bonds; it is about “Blitzscaling,” monopolies, and burning cash to capture a market.

But take a more philosophical view: Venture Capital is literally the hyper-concentrated private ownership of the future. It is a “bet on the future” mentality at its core.

  • In a sense, everyone is betting against some odds in the future, isn’t it?

So, how does that work? Some countries have realized that the fastest way to achieve national prosperity isn’t just through five-year central plans for steel production, but by funding a 34-year-old who thinks they can disrupt a 100-year-old industry.

As contradictory as it seems for a collective society to embrace the Power Law, the calculation works simply and beautifully: We need outliers to pull the average up.

The Mad Math

My entry into this world wasn’t through a boardroom, but a failure.

A few years ago, I had an app idea. Got a friend from work, spent $1,000, and tried to build it. To be honest, considering what I made at the time, this was a crazy bet.

  • My cost of living—rent included—was less than $500 USD a month. I didn’t just bet money; I bet two months of survival.

Sure, I did the research. I talked to people. I did the textbook things. But ultimately, it failed. Zero traction.

At the time, I felt like I had set money on fire. But looking back at the data, I realize I was just a statistic.

  • According to research, 42% of startups fail simply due to “lack of market need.” I had built a solution looking for a problem—a failure of what the HSGL (Heart, Smarts, Guts, Luck) framework calls “Smarts”: the inability to validate a hypothesis before spending cash.

But that $1,000 was valuable tuition. It introduced me to the concept of Asymmetric Risk-Reward.

In a normal job, risk is symmetrical: you work an hour, you get paid an hour. This means you are stuck in the linear trap—the “rat race.” You can never work 1,000 hours in a single hour, there will always be a celling.

Venture Capital breaks the physics of time because it operates on a Power Law distribution.

Taking a high-risk investment—even one with a 99% chance of failing—is rational if the potential payoff is 1,000%. You can lose $1,000 ten times in a row, but if the eleventh bet returns $50,000, you are winning.

This is why I still set aside a portion of my annual income for “risky” assets. I am not gambling; I am paying for tickets to the Power Law lottery (okay, to be honest, it is still gambling), knowing full well that the vast majority of my tickets are mathematically destined for the “Valley of Death”—that brutal period between years 2 and 5 where 70% of startups die.

The “Right Stuff” and The Myth of the Dropout

This brings me to today. Comfortable job. Portfolio is stable. If I just lay low, I will eventually be wealthy.

Charlie Munger once said the first $100k is a bitch, but the rest is easy. He was right. That first milestone was a grind, especially coming from a developing country, but the next $100k took a fraction of the time.

So why risk it? Why try to become a VC or join one? Comfort is a sedative. When you are safe, the hunger usually dies.

Tom Wolfe wrote a famous book called The Right Stuff about test pilots who strapped themselves into experimental rockets that had a high probability of exploding. They didn’t do it for the money. They did it for the “zing”—the need to push the outside of the envelope.

There is a pervasive myth that you need to be a 20-year-old dropout to have this “zing.” But the data from Ali Tamaseb’s Super Founders study tells a different story. The median age of a billion-dollar founder is 34. They aren’t usually kids; they are people who have seen the world and realized it can be built differently.

Venture Capital is about leaving the spectator stands. It’s about understanding that while only 0.1% of companies become unicorns, the only way to be part of that story is to be in the arena.

I am not the one in the rocket—that’s the founder. But I want to be the one building the launchpad. Even here, some of us just have the itch to push the envelope. We don’t want to just own the means of production; we want to fund the people inventing new ones.
