I have always been a “Move Fast and Break Things” fundamentalist until this week

My entire career has been built on the religion of the startup: Ship the MVP. Iterate. Fail fast. If you aren’t embarrassed by your first release, you shipped too late. To me, speed was the only metric that mattered. “Perfect” was the enemy of “Done.”

By all means, we live in the era of the “MVP” (Minimum Viable Product). Where we build things to be shipped, tested, and often, discarded. We upgrade our phones every two years; we rewrite our codebases every five.

This week, I had a chat with a Senior Architect—someone who has been building software since before I was born. I was pitching him on a rapid iteration strategy, explaining how we could patch things later.

  • “You are building for the next sprint,” he said. “I am building for the next decades.”

If he builds this wrong today, five years from now, someone I have never met will lose their weekend trying to fix it. True quality isn’t about how fast you ship; it’s about how long it matters.

That conversation stuck with me. It gnawed at my “speed-first” worldview. It forced me to look outside my bubble of agile sprints and quarterly OKRs to look at things that were built with a completely different philosophy.

  • There is a quiet, expensive dignity in building things that last.

I’ve been thinking about this lately through the lens of very different industries: enterprise software, modern motorcycles, and historical manufacturing. On the surface, they have nothing in common. But if you look under the hood, they tell the same story: Time is the only true test of quality.

The Historical Echo

This tension between “absolute quality” and “sufficient quantity” isn’t new. It is perhaps best illustrated by the engineering dichotomy of World War II tank manufacturing.

Setting aside the politics and the war itself, if you look strictly at the manufacturing mindsets, you see two opposing religions.

The German Approach (The Tiger/Panther): This was the perfection mindset taken to the extreme. German engineers treated a 60-ton tank like a Swiss watch. The welds were perfectly machined. The tolerances were incredibly tight. They used complex interleaved road wheels to ensure the smoothest possible ride for the gunner. It was an engineering marvel, designed for perfection. It marched from the factories in Germany to the front line, no problem.

The Russian Approach (The T-34): This was the ultimate “MVP.” The Russian philosophy was that a tank didn’t need to last for 10 years; it only needed to last for the few weeks it would survive on the front line. Things even broke down some miles before getting there.

  • The exterior welds were rough and often unfinished.

  • The driver’s hatch was heavy and crude.

  • There were gaps in the armor plating that wouldn’t pass a German inspection, but they didn’t stop the tank from fighting.

    • The armor plating in some cases was taken from other machines/cars, as well as far as I recall, it was essentially school project tanks.

Both are perfect for their use case. But while the Russian approach (Quantity) won the war of attrition. The German approach (Quality) won the admiration of engineers.

The 50-Year Codebase

If you work in tech, it’s fashionable to complain about old tech companies like SAP, I mean, we made a category call old tech to begin with. The screens can look dated; the implementation is heavy.

But consider this: SAP was founded in 1972 in Germany.

There are SAP systems running today that manage the payroll of nations and the supply chains of the world’s largest companies. When you sign a contract with SAP, you aren’t just buying software; you are entering a marriage. There are cases where they offer maintenance timelines that span 30 years.

In the startup world, we celebrate a “3-year exit.” In the world of deep quality, they plan for a 30-year relationship. Building a product that can survive five decades of technological upheaval without breaking the core business logic is a staggering feat of engineering. It is the digital equivalent of a Gothic cathedral: built slowly, stone by stone, intended to stand long after the architects are gone.

The Motorcycle Story

I recently fell to a YouTube rabbit hole watching a mechanic strip down three motorcycles side-by-side. They were all in the same “cc” segment, but they came from three different worlds: China, Japan, and Italy.

On paper, the specs were similar. But the Italian bike costs double the price of the Japanese one, and the Chinese bikes are dirt cheap. At first glance, it seemed like a “luxury tax.” But then the mechanic started taking them apart, and I was amazed.

The nuance here is important: All three bikes were good. The Chinese bike wasn’t junk; it was a solid machine that would run for many years. The Japanese bike was a marvel of efficient mass production.

But the Italian bike revealed why it cost double. It wasn’t about the badge; it was about the obsession with quality.

  • The Components: The Italian manufacturer didn’t just “make” a bike; they curated it using specialized components (Brembo, Marzocchi) from companies that have perfected their craft for a century. The oldest components provider dates back to 1900-ish.

  • The “Over-the-Top” Detail: The moment that truly shocked me was the wiring harness. On the Chinese bike, the cables were bundled with simple Velcro strips. Functional? Yes.

    • But on the Italian bike? They used a high-grade, fire-resistant sheathing. Not just heat-resistant—fire-resistant. Carefully wrap each cable; a cable is something that can’t be machine-wrapped in this case.

Is it strictly necessary for a street bike to have fireproof wiring wrappers? Probably not. But that is the difference. One was built to “work fine.” The other was built to an internal standard of excellence that refused to compromise.

The Ecosystem of Excellence

What connects the German software giant, the Italian motorcycle atelier, and the over-engineered steel of the past?

When we look at that Italian bike today, or that 50-year-old SAP code, we are seeing the modern descendants of that “Tiger” mindset. It is an approach that says: We are not building this just to get it out the door. We are building this to be the best possible version of itself, regardless of the cost or the effort.

For years, I looked at premium pricing and assumed it was marketing. But this teardown changed my mind. The price is justified.

You are paying for that extra 20% of effort that makes no logical sense on a spreadsheet but makes all the difference in the soul of the product.

  • It’s the SAP engineer refactoring a kernel of code to ensure it’s backward compatible with a database from 1995.

  • It’s the Italian engineer choosing a fire-resistant wrapper instead of a plastic tie because what if?

We often mistake “expensive” for “overpriced.” But sometimes, expensive is just the honest cost of not cutting corners. In a world of disposable tech and fast fashion, there is a quiet dignity in building things that are intended to survive the fire.

Time applied the same rule for quality, I was a strong believer of “either it should be fast and prepared to be broken, or it should take time and be really stable”, time will tell which lasts. But maybe I’m changing, sure, make it fast enough, but remember we are building for the next decades.
