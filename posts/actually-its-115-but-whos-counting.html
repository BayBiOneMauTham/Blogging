Actually, it’s 11,5, but who's counting

Last year was… a lot. Too many things shifted at once—politics, conflicts, technology, even the climate. The U.S. welcomed a new President, and the world collectively held its breath, wondering what would come next. Through it all, life carried on, but if you zoom out, it feels like we’re inching closer and closer to the edge.

One strange reminder of that looming edge is something you might not think about often: the Doomsday Clock. Yes, it’s still around. Yes, it’s still ticking. And yes, it’s now closer to midnight than ever before.

For context, the Clock was first set in 1947 at 7 minutes to midnight, intended as a symbolic warning of humanity's nearness to catastrophic self-destruction. Since then, it has been reset backward 8 times and forward 18 times.

  • The farthest we’ve ever been from annihilation? 17 minutes to midnight in 1991, following the end of the Cold War.

  • The closest? 89 seconds to Midnight, set in January 2025.

Oh, I went off track, did I?

Back at it

One thing I’ve learned—whether you’re joining a startup, a mid-size company, or a giant—is this: you must know what you want, and what the company is actually offering.

This realization hit me hard when I looked around and noticed how many people, usually with <3 YOEs or fresh out of school, seemed completely lost about what big tech is really about. The brand names are shiny, the perks sound amazing, and the salary looks like a golden ticket.

But here’s the truth I wish more people would hear:

  • Big tech is not built for learning the basics.

Yeah, it offers a lot of learning, but it’s not designed for entry-level discovery.

If you’re still learning how the working world operates, how to collaborate, or how to build foundational skills, jumping into a massive, highly specialized machine may leave you feeling like a small cog rather than someone who’s actually growing.

Only when you reach a certain level of understanding, only then, you get here and ride the wave.

What You Really Learn at Big Tech (From my lens)

Being inside a massive, global organization is nothing like what I imagined earlier in my career. I thought I had it figured out — I did my research, I read the articles, I asked around.

  • But as the saying goes: “Everyone has a plan until they get punched in the face.” - Mike Tyson.

At this level, the game is different — and the lessons you pick up can reshape the way you think about work, growth, and even money itself.

1. The Bird’s-Eye View

The peak of working in big tech is the vantage point you get. It’s not just about bigger teams, fancier tools, or global offices — it’s about perspective.

Suddenly, data isn’t just numbers in a dashboard or a market report you could buy off the shelf. It’s alive. It’s flowing in from every direction: customers in different geographies, enterprise partners with their own strategies, regulators with shifting priorities, and global markets that can turn overnight.

  • And it’s not just the volume of data — it’s the quality.

This vantage point changes you. It forces you to connect dots that you didn’t even realize existed before. You stop thinking small, in terms of “What does my product need today?” and you begin thinking on a global scale:

  • How do geopolitical shifts affect product adoption?

  • How do changes in regulation cascade across multiple markets?

  • Where is everyone moving to?

It’s a kind of strategic muscle you don’t develop until you’re there. And once you’ve seen the world through that lens, it becomes almost impossible to go back to the narrower way of working.

  • And then you start to hear company names from the Valley as a partner, something you hardly come by working on a local level.

2. Scale comes with problems

I’ve done team-building, collaboration, and change management before — but at this level, it’s a whole new game. In smaller environments, you can sometimes improvise or move fast without too much worry.

  • In a massive global organization, even the tiniest shift can create ripples across.

That’s where you begin to understand the real meaning of rigor. Standards, terminology, and compliance aren’t just words tossed around in meetings; they’re the foundation that keeps the machine running without collapsing under its own weight.

  • Within all that, the chance to do something new feels like winning the lottery

Still, I worked on a brand-new, innovative project. In a place where most people are assigned to maintain and optimize already-successful engines, being able to help build something fresh is like striking gold. People would kill to get onto these projects because they know that when innovation happens inside big tech, the ripple effects can be massive.

  • And then the dependencies take months to align.

    • And more to complete.

  • People joining the project come from 3 different continents in multiple waves.

  • 5 hrs meeting time per day is the norm.

  • Mr. WeekendWork and Ms. NightMeetings are not strangers.

A product born here doesn’t just serve one market — it has the potential to change the rules for an entire industry.

So when you’re part of kicking one off, it feels almost impossible, like moving a single gear and somehow propelling a machine the size of the world. Read THIS

The number googol equals 1.0 x 10,100, or 1 followed by 100 zeros. To put that into perspective, scientists theorize there are between 120 and 300 sextillion stars in the observable universe, which translates into around 1,078 to 1,082 of the number of atoms in said universe. Daniel de Bruin's gear reduction machine would theoretically take more power to turn the last gear one revolution than the universe could provide.

There are a total of 100 gears in his machine, with each gear pair having a reduction of 1 to 10, so for every 10 revolutions, the gear next in line makes one rotation, and so forth. So for the last gear to spin one time, the first has to revolve a googol number of times.

3. Following the Money Flow

This one hit me hardest last year because it resonated beyond just my role — it reshaped the way I think about money, investment, and growth in my personal life.

  • Here, you don’t just see projects as isolated roadmaps with features and deadlines.

You begin to see how money really flows: It’s the strategic portfolio management. Some bets are made with quick returns in mind, others are long-term strategic plays, and the mix has to balance risk and reward across the entire company.

Multi-million-dollar projects are multi-asset portfolios. The stakes are much higher than a stock you can just sell tomorrow. Projects come with people, reputations, brand trust, and strategic positioning — so decisions carry weight. You can’t just pull out; you have to think about timing, transition, and the collateral impact across the ecosystem.

It’s not the same as “put some money in the market and check your app.”

  • You start thinking in terms of how global interest rates influence investment priorities, how regulatory shifts shape entire product lines, and how 3–5 year projections determine today’s funding decisions.

  • You start to appreciate that some assets — and some projects — don’t come and go easily. They are tied to long-term commitments, relationships, and infrastructure.

What’s next?

So yeah — that’s what the last 11.5 months felt like.

A year of standing on top of a mountain of gears, trying to nudge one forward while the whole world seemed to shift beneath my feet. Exhausting? Absolutely. Worth it? More than I thought possible.

And if I’ve learned anything, it’s this: big tech isn’t a starship academy. There’s no AI oracle to map your destiny, no wormhole shortcut that tells you exactly who you’ll become here. What you do get is perspective, discipline, and the rare chance to tinker with gears so massive they make galaxies look small.

Now, the year ahead? Well… let’s see if I can finally give that last gear a push. And hopefully, it won’t take a googol rotations.

Disclaimer

This isn’t a glamorized take on big tech life.

Quite the opposite—I want to be clear that it’s not easy, def not a lay-back job, and most definitely not for everyone.

If anyone is telling you it’s easy here, then the project they work on is mostly in their maturity.
Yeah, sure, we do have that, but there are certain cost that come with it. Go figure what.

So please, know what you want first, understand both the ups and the downs. If you’re good with that, then by all means, consider big tech.
