Why Knowledge Access Still Shapes Global Incomes

Last week, I searched for a book on behavioral economics—something you’d expect to find easily. In the U.S., it appeared on Amazon for US$18, with next‐day delivery. In my home country? It wasn’t available locally.

I would have needed to pay US$50 or more (including shipping and customs) or wait 30+ days for an international order. And, the important “and” is that I don’t even have that option.

At first glance, it seems trivial. But beneath it lies a structural pattern: when your access to up-to-date books, research, tools, and networks is delayed or expensive, you’re operating with a handicap. Over a lifetime, over the number of people it impacts, those delays compound.

Income Gaps: A Quick Reality Check

To ground this in numbers:

  • The World Bank notes that the average person in a high‐income economy has GNI per capita well above US$13,935, while many low-income economies fall far below. (See here )

  • According to the World Inequality Database, the richest 10% of the global population now earn ~52% of total global income, while the bottom 50% earn only ~8.5%. (World Inequality Report 2022)
    These figures reflect more than just capital or natural resources—they reflect underlying productivity, innovation, network effects, and yes, access to knowledge.

The Compound Engine of Income

Imagine two aspiring engineers—one in Silicon Valley, one in a small city in a developing country. Both are hardworking and smart. But one has instant access to:

  • The latest research papers

  • Cutting-edge books

  • Online communities

  • Quick purchasing of tools and equipment

The other? Must wait for shipping, deals with limited library access, fewer mentors, higher import costs, and perhaps slower internet.

Over 10 years, that difference becomes a network-effect advantage:

  • Faster learning → higher skill

  • More visibility → better job offers

  • More innovation → higher income

It’s not just what you know—it’s how fast you can know it, apply it, and iterate. Speed matters.

Why Access Still Harrows the Gap

Several forces keep this knowledge-access gap persistent:

  1. Price and supply hurdles
    Books, courses, and tools in many countries cost more (import duties, shipping) or may not be available in the local language. That means extra cost, delay, or both.

  2. Networks and communities
    Being in a hub (US/UK/Western Europe) means access to mentors, peer groups, and industry events. Outside those hubs, the feedback loop is weaker.

  3. Infrastructure & licensing
    Universities and institutions in richer countries often subscribe to global research libraries, tools, and premium datasets. Many in lower-income economies cannot easily afford those.

  4. Language & localization
    Most of the big tech books, research papers, and frameworks are published in English, sometimes with region-specific localization lagging months or years behind.

  5. Speed of adoption
    In global technology and science, being first matters. If you’re operating one or two years behind the frontier, you’re playing catch-up instead of innovating ahead.

Country-Level Patterns

Again, the data backs it up:

  • A country with a higher GDP per capita often correlates with faster adoption of new technologies, higher education access, and stronger institutions. (Our World in Data)

  • One study looking at “income per capita across countries” found that, for many less advanced economies, even under the most optimistic scenario, catching up can take decades (27 to 194 years or more) unless the knowledge base is strengthened. (ResearchGate)
    In plain English: If your country doesn’t have strong access to global knowledge, an innovation culture, and the networks of learning, bridging the gap becomes very slow.

So What Can We Do?

This isn’t a fatalist piece—it’s a wake-up call. Some things that help:

  • Invest locally in libraries, university subscriptions, and open-access research. I am a huge advocate for freedom of access to information.

  • Encourage know-how mobility: scholarships, cross-border internships, knowledge networks.

  • Use digital wisely: Yes, the internet helps—but only if costs, latency, and licensing don’t leave you behind.

  • Focus on absorptive capacity, not just content: being able to apply what you learn is crucial.

  • Time matters: Every year you’re delayed is a compounding loss in opportunity.

Yeah, it’s just that

The richest currency in the world isn’t the dollar.
It’s the ability to access knowledge, apply it, iterate on it—and do so faster than your peers.
Until every serious learner—wherever they are born—has near-instant, low-friction access to global knowledge, access will remain a structural advantage for some and a barrier for others. The gap might not widen overnight, but it quietly compounds. And that’s the invisible divide.
