What comes to mind when I ask you to picture the “perfect” education?

For most, it’s a very specific aesthetic: Straight A’s. Private school. An Ivy League acceptance letter. Maybe a full-ride scholarship to a top-tier US or EU university, culminating in a PhD.

Now, what about a “bad” education?

A remote village school? Homeschooling? A resume built entirely on Coursera certificates instead of a formal diploma? Even a Liberal Arts degree gets thrown into this bucket by the hyper-pragmatic tech crowd.

We all have variations of our ideal educational path, but the silent agreement in the corporate world is this: an Ivy League diploma is worth a thousand Coursera certificates.

It’s not really about the education in a strict sense; it’s about the prestige that comes with it. If you want to sit in the C-suite, you better have an MBA. If you don’t have one yet, an Exec-MBA from a brand-name university is the accepted shortcut. If you’re already someone important and lack the time, you just wait for a university to hand you an Honorary Doctorate.

It’s not a secret that the world prefers people with official, institutionalized recognition. I don’t hate traditional education—in fact, I’m well aware that an elite MBA program offers network access and signaling that you simply cannot download. It is the easiest way to prove to a board (or a VC) that you have been vetted and are worth the investment.

But things are changing rapidly, and traditional schooling might not be the undisputed king of value much longer.

The Master’s Degree Epiphany

This train of thought started recently. Over the last few months, I was seriously looking into pursuing a Master’s degree. I was mapping out timelines for late 2026, comparing traditional MBAs, Executive MBAs, and even specialized technical programs like Business Information Systems. I was trying to figure out which letters after my name would best position me for fast-track leadership roles or executive programs.

But the deeper I got into the brochures and tuition fees, the more I hesitated. I realized the ROI just wasn’t adding up.

I had to separate the idea of the diploma from the actual value of the education received. If we look past the textbook definition of education (”the process of receiving systematic instruction at a university”), we arrive at Plato’s take: Education is the process of training the body and soul to achieve perfection, virtue, and knowledge of the truth.

In a pure sense, learning is just about training yourself to understand new things so you can be a highly productive, capable human. And right now, the university is no longer the only—or even the best—place to do that.

A Brief History of “Monkey See, Monkey Do”

To understand where education is breaking, you have to look at how it was built.

1. The Primate Phase (Pre-1500s): For most of human history, learning was just “monkey see, monkey do.” It was an oral tradition and an apprenticeship. You learned a trade by watching a master do the trade. Ancient civilizations (Egypt, India, Greece) had formal schools for scribes and priests, leaning heavily on the Socratic method of critical inquiry. It was intimate and highly practical.

Plato was here, with his own concept of education.

2. The Industrial Factory (1500s–1900s): As populations exploded, we needed mass education. Governments established compulsory schooling to create literate, compliant citizens. The classroom was born. It relied heavily on rote memorization, standardized testing, and teacher-centric lecturing. It was highly effective at raising the global baseline of literacy, but the cost was conformity.

School, as we know it, began here.

3. The Psychological Shift (Early 20th Century): Educators like John Dewey and Maria Montessori realized that treating children like hard drives to be written on was flawed. The progressive movement pushed for child-centered, active learning, integrating psychology (Piaget) to focus on how human brains actually engage with information.

Modern preschool education ideas began here.

4. The Digital Age (Present): Now, we have EdTech, project-based learning, and MOOCs. The internet decentralized the classroom.

The traditional school model worked. It scaled human knowledge. But its fundamental flaw is that it forces a standardized pace onto unstandardized minds.

And this is where things get interesting. We went full circle

The Next Decade

Technology is booming, and it offers a solution to the bottlenecks of the past. AI provides an off-balance advantage for pure learning that no university can match.

  • Absolute Personalization: An AI tutor adapts to your exact level of understanding. If you don’t get a concept, it rephrases it infinitely without judgment—something standardized schooling fundamentally lacks.

  • Omnipresence: It is always there. Unlike a professor with office hours, it doesn’t sleep.

  • Limitless Knowledge on Demand: Imagine needing to understand a complex financial model or a new coding framework, and having all the perfectly synthesized information delivered to you in two minutes.

  • Zero Marginal Cost: It is effectively free compared to a $100,000 degree.

The Catch:

Of course, there are massive trade-offs. You don’t get to build a high-level human network chatting with an LLM. You have to be ruthlessly self-motivated to learn outside of a structured syllabus. And the biggest hurdle: No one believes you actually learned it. Currently, there is no prestige or societal trust attached to “I learned this from an AI.”

The Future Institutional Model

Because raw knowledge is now universally accessible and functionally free, the business model of the educational institution has to change. If they are just selling access to information, they are obsolete.

Universities of the future will need to pivot to offer what cannot be learned in a bedroom:

  • The Un-Digitizable: Deep social skills, physical training, complex human collaboration, and high-stakes networking.

  • The Guardrails: They will transition from being teachers of knowledge to verifiers of knowledge. They will offer the testing, the crucible, and the certification that proves you actually know what you claim.

  • Hyper-Specialization: Programs focused on cutting-edge physical research (robotics, biotech, material sciences) that require multi-million dollar labs.

The Speed of Creation vs. Consumption

Things are moving terrifyingly fast. Over the last couple of months, it has become obvious that keeping up with new tech is a full-time job. AI models are being released every other weekend; the nature of work shifts monthly; you learn a new framework only to have a newer, better one replace it immediately.

Education—both institutional and personal—needs to catch up. Our speed of adoption must equal our speed of creation.

If we don’t fix the way we learn, within the next couple of generations, we are going to see a massive, insurmountable gap. It will be the ultimate class divide: not between the rich and the poor, but between the Creators who create & leverage these tools, and the Consumers who just scroll past the outputs.
